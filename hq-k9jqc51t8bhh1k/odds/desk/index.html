    The plan

    Read this first
    Against the spread is a coin flip by construction: the spread is the market's median and my model cannot beat it (encompassing coefficient −0.02, p = 0.90, 855 games). Your pool locks the spread when you submit, so the number you get is the one on your screen — and the only edge is when that number is better than the market's. Each half point is worth the games that land on the points in between: in the NFL 15.0% land on exactly 3 and 9.1% on 7 (6,969 games, 1999–2025). So: for each game the table shows DraftKings' number now and the number at which each side becomes worth taking. Type what your pool shows and the box gives the call. Same number as the market: flip a coin. A better one: take it. A worse one: wait, or take the other side if it is better there.

    Lineups across all games

    The full-slate contest: nine players drawn from every game on the card. Cash lineups maximise the raw projection. Tournament lineups stack a quarterback with his own receivers — the same touchdown pays both, which raises variance, and variance is what you need when only the top 1% cashes.

    Line shopping — the part that actually pays

    Four sportsbooks now quote college, where until today there was one. On a spread the number matters as much as the price, because college margins pile up on a few values: 10.6% of games end on exactly 3 and 8.8% on exactly 7, measured over 3,960 FBS games. Getting +3 instead of +2.5 turns a loss into a push on every one of those — worth more than the bookmaker's entire cut. This does not tell you which side to take. It tells you where to place the bet you already wanted.

    The softest market, and the least measurable
    Props get a fraction of the attention sides do, which is why this is the one place worth hunting. But FanDuel posts them one-sided — only the “yes” price exists — so the bookmaker's cut cannot be stripped out the way it can on a two-sided market. Every edge below is an upper bound. Typical prop vig runs 6–8 points — but that range has no measurement behind it in this project. The only prop cut actually measured here is the two-sided NFL one at 6.5%, and a one-sided quote cannot be de-vigged at all. An earlier figure of 10.8 points was retracted. Treat anything under roughly 7 points as noise, and treat the threshold itself as unverified.

    How these are priced

    Not against a season average — that comparison was made on 31 August and overstated the gap, because a hitter facing a weak starter genuinely is better than his season line. Each player's number here is the share of games in which he actually did the thing, pulled from his real game logs, then adjusted for tonight's opposing starter using the standard hitter-versus-pitcher formula.

    One correction worth knowing about: RBI chances were first computed with a formula that treats runs batted in as independent arrivals. They are not — a three-RBI game is one game with an RBI, not three. Checked against real logs, the formula said 48.2% where the truth was 40.1%. Every RBI “edge” it produced was that error. These now count games instead.

    How long before any of this is knowable
    At a median price of −114 you must win 53.27% just to break even. To prove a real edge at 80% power takes 432 graded bets if the true rate is 60%, 876 if 58%, 2,631 if 56%, 6,556 if 55%. At roughly 25 bets a week that is 17 weeks, 35 weeks, or two years. A good Sunday tells you nothing, and so does a bad one.
    And picking the single biggest edge out of these is picking the one most likely to be wrong. The calibration error on the priced markets is about 2 points, so a gap of that size is not noise — it is the model genuinely believing something the market does not. This project has never yet found the market to be the wrong side of one of those. These are disagreements, logged so they can be graded. They are not recommendations.

    Retracted

    “Strikeouts do most of the work”

    Stated from a point estimate with no error bar. With bootstrapped intervals, strikeout rate cannot be distinguished from zero on 4,369 games. This card used to end “walk rate survives; strikeouts do not”. That was withdrawn. Correcting for testing many things at once, walk rate does not survive either (p = 0.077), and under the corrected ablation rule strikeout rate is kept. The honest reading is that neither can be separated from the other on this sample.

    “The park effect is noise”

    Half right, and the wrong half — and then that correction was itself withdrawn. The claim that ballpark is actively harmful rested on an ablation rule that was inverted; corrected, ballpark is no decision. It was NOT removed from the model and is still in mlb_predict.py, which is the right outcome for a term the evidence cannot settle either way.

    “Line shopping is the proven edge”

    Repeated for four days on the strength of daily snapshots and a proxy. It has no backtest. Reworded to what the evidence supports: shopping reduces the loss.

    “The market beats the model”

    Said for four days from point estimates, with the validation season pooled into the test season. Split properly with a date-blocked paired bootstrap, every interval crosses zero. Withdrawn.

    “Home field contributes nothing”

    A [0,0] interval was a bug, not a null. Home teams win 52.86% but score only +0.73% more runs — because they do not bat in the ninth when leading. A run-total model structurally cannot see MLB home field. This is also the mechanism for totals being the only significant losing market.

    “Walk rate is a confirmed keeper”

    Does not survive Benjamini-Hochberg (p = 0.077). Roughly 50 tests were run across this project with no multiplicity correction at all until now.

    “1,100 bets proves a 3-point edge”

    Wrong by 57%. It is 1,717 one-sided, 2,180 two-sided at 80% power. For the NFL that is 22 seasons, not eleven.

    “Six tests, it never beat the market once”

    Too strong, and it flattened a real distinction. Of the six: three show the market clearly ahead (NFL miss, NFL encompassing, college miss — all intervals exclude zero). Two cannot separate model from market (both baseball splits). One, the betting simulation, is statistically empty. Football is settled; baseball is not.

    The −$526 betting simulation

    1,138 bets at $100 flat carries a standard error near $3,373 — an interval of roughly −$7,100 to +$6,100 — and used the same games as the log-loss test, so it was never independent evidence. Removed from the scorecard.

    Bugs found by testing, not by luck

    The model was frozen

    Its data stopped on 29 August while it kept answering questions about September games. Nothing was scheduled to refresh it. Now runs daily at 7:47am with a consistency gate that fails loudly if the two files disagree.

    Live prices leaking in

    Odds captured mid-game look like enormous value on a team already losing 8–1. Five independent gates now catch them; all four real live prices from that day were rejected and both genuine pregame prices kept.

    A gate that deleted the point

    The disagreement rule threw out a game 4h38m before first pitch — and book disagreement is exactly where shopping value lives. It was protecting the tool by removing its purpose. Disagreement alone can no longer reject anything.
